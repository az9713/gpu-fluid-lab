1 · Black-Hole Ray Tracer

Bend light itself: a real-time general-relativistic renderer of a spinning black hole.

General Relativity Differential Geometry · ODEs WebGL / GLSL

What it is

An interactive renderer that shows what a camera orbiting a rotating (Kerr) black hole would actually see: the warped, doubled image of the accretion disk arching over the shadow, the thin bright photon ring, starfields smeared by gravitational lensing, and the disk glowing hotter and bluer on the side spinning toward you. This is the physics behind the visuals in Interstellar — but computed live, with sliders for black-hole spin, disk temperature, camera inclination and orbit. In plain terms: instead of drawing straight lines from the camera into the scene (ordinary ray tracing), every single ray is a curve, because gravity bends the path light takes. The renderer's job is to integrate each curved path correctly, millions of times per frame, on the GPU.

The science

A rotating black hole is described by the Kerr metric — the exact solution of Einstein's field equations for a spinning mass, found by Roy Kerr in 1963. In Boyer–Lindquist coordinates \((t, r, \theta, \phi)\), with mass \(M\) and spin parameter \(a = J/M\):

$$ds^2 = -\left(1 - \frac{2Mr}{\Sigma}\right)dt^2 - \frac{4Mar\sin^2\theta}{\Sigma}\,dt\,d\phi + \frac{\Sigma}{\Delta}dr^2 + \Sigma\, d\theta^2 + \left(r^2 + a^2 + \frac{2Ma^2 r \sin^2\theta}{\Sigma}\right)\sin^2\theta \, d\phi^2$$

where \(\Sigma = r^2 + a^2\cos^2\theta\) and \(\Delta = r^2 - 2Mr + a^2\).

Light rays are null geodesics of this metric — the straightest possible paths through curved spacetime, satisfying the geodesic equation:

$$\frac{d^2 x^\mu}{d\lambda^2} + \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}\frac{dx^\alpha}{d\lambda}\frac{dx^\beta}{d\lambda} = 0$$

The Kerr geometry is special: thanks to its symmetries there are enough conserved quantities — energy \(E\), axial angular momentum \(L_z\), and the surprising fourth constant, the Carter constant \(Q\) — to reduce the ray equations to first-order form. That's a huge computational gift: fewer equations, better numerical stability, and a built-in correctness check (the constants must stay constant along the ray; if they drift, the integrator is wrong).

Three physical effects make the image spectacular, and all fall straight out of the math:

  • Gravitational lensing. Light passing near the hole is deflected so strongly you see the far side of the accretion disk bent above and below the shadow, plus higher-order images from rays that loop around once, twice, … producing the nested photon ring near \(r = 3M\) (for zero spin).
  • Relativistic Doppler beaming. Disk gas orbits at a substantial fraction of light speed. The approaching side gets brighter by a factor \(\delta^{3}\) (with \(\delta\) the Doppler factor) and shifts blue; the receding side dims and reddens. One side of the disk visibly blazes.
  • Gravitational redshift. Light climbing out of the potential well loses energy, so the inner disk edge is reddened and dimmed by the factor \(\sqrt{-g_{tt}}\) along the path.

Disk color is physical, not painted: each disk annulus radiates as a blackbody with the temperature profile of a Novikov–Thorne thin disk, \(T(r) \propto r^{-3/4}\) far out, and the received spectrum is shifted by the combined Doppler + gravitational factor \(g = \nu_{\text{obs}}/\nu_{\text{emit}}\), with intensity transforming as \(I_\nu / \nu^3 = \text{invariant}\).

The computation

  • Each pixel spawns a ray integrated backwards from the camera through curved spacetime, using an adaptive Runge–Kutta–Fehlberg (RK45) integrator written in GLSL — step size shrinks automatically near the hole where curvature is violent, stretches far away where space is nearly flat.
  • Termination logic per ray: hit the disk (accumulate emission), fall through the horizon (\(r_+ = M + \sqrt{M^2 - a^2}\), render black), or escape to infinity (sample the background starfield).
  • Conserved-quantity drift (\(E, L_z, Q\)) is monitored as a live accuracy metric — the test suite asserts drift below tolerance across a grid of camera positions and spins.
  • Production scope: preset tours (Schwarzschild, near-extremal Kerr \(a = 0.998M\), edge-on Interstellar view), camera-path animation with video export, and an explainer overlay that labels the photon ring, the lensed far-side disk, and the beamed bright side as you look at them.

2 · Market Microstructure Observatory

A living, breathing limit order book — simulated from first principles, rendered like weather.

Market Microstructure Stochastic Processes · Queueing WebGL · Agent-Based Simulation

What it is

A full electronic-exchange-in-miniature: a matching engine implementing real price–time priority, a population of algorithmic trading agents (market makers, momentum traders, mean-reverters, noise traders) submitting and cancelling orders, and on top of it a visualization layer that treats the order book like a landscape — a 3D depth surface evolving through time, trade executions as particle bursts, liquidity evaporating and refilling like tide. In plain terms: real markets are queues of resting buy and sell orders at each price; every price chart you've ever seen is the thin trace left behind by this much richer object. This project simulates the whole object and lets you watch it.

The science

Market microstructure has a set of stylized facts — statistical regularities every real market exhibits and that any credible simulator must reproduce. They are the project's validation targets:

  • Fat-tailed returns. Price changes are wildly non-Gaussian: the distribution of returns has power-law tails, \(P(|r| > x) \sim x^{-\alpha}\) with \(\alpha \approx 3\) — extreme moves are orders of magnitude more common than a normal distribution predicts.
  • Volatility clustering. Big moves follow big moves: the autocorrelation of absolute returns \(\text{corr}(|r_t|, |r_{t+\tau}|)\) decays slowly (roughly as a power law), while returns themselves are nearly uncorrelated. Calm and stormy periods bunch together.
  • Square-root market impact. Executing a large order of volume \(Q\) moves the price not linearly but as $$\Delta p \;\approx\; Y\,\sigma \sqrt{\tfrac{Q}{V}}$$ (with \(\sigma\) volatility, \(V\) daily volume) — one of the most robust empirical laws in finance, and remarkably, it emerges from simple order-flow dynamics rather than being imposed.
  • Long-memory order flow. Buy orders follow buys and sells follow sells, with autocorrelation persisting over thousands of trades — yet prices stay near-unpredictable, because market makers adjust quotes to compensate. The tension between these two facts is the deepest result in the field.

The theoretical backbone is the zero-intelligence order-book model (Farmer, Smith et al.): even agents placing random orders — arrivals as Poisson processes with rate \(\lambda\), cancellations at rate \(\delta\), market orders at rate \(\mu\) — generate realistic spreads and depth profiles. Dimensional analysis of these rates predicts the average spread:

$$s \;\sim\; \frac{\mu}{\lambda}\, f\!\left(\frac{\delta}{\mu}\right)$$

Layering strategic agents on this baseline — market makers managing inventory risk (skewing quotes as position accumulates, the Avellaneda–Stoikov framework, where reservation price shifts by \(q\gamma\sigma^2(T-t)\) for inventory \(q\) and risk aversion \(\gamma\)), momentum and mean-reversion traders — turns the statistics from merely plausible to quantitatively realistic, and lets you run experiments: What happens to the book in a flash crash? How does liquidity vanish when market makers pull quotes simultaneously?

The computation

  • Matching engine: price–time priority with limit, market, cancel, and immediate-or-cancel order types; \(O(1)\) best-quote access, \(O(\log n)\) insertion. Verified with a property-based test suite (no negative spreads ever, volume conservation, priority never violated, deterministic replay from an event log).
  • Agent scheduler: discrete-event simulation over a single event clock — hundreds of agents, millions of events per run, fully reproducible from a seed.
  • Validation dashboard: live QQ-plots of returns against Gaussian, tail-exponent estimation (Hill estimator), autocorrelation curves of \(r_t\) vs \(|r_t|\), and measured impact vs the square-root law — the sim ships with its own evidence.
  • Visual centerpieces: the 3D book-depth surface scrolling through time like a mountain range; order-flow particles streaming into the book and annihilating at the mid-price; a flash-crash replay mode with time-dilation slider.

3 · GPU Fluid Dynamics Lab

The Navier–Stokes equations, solved sixty times a second, painted in smoke and vortices.

Computational Fluid Dynamics Nonlinear PDEs WebGPU Compute

What it is

A real-time two-dimensional fluid simulator running entirely on the GPU: inject dye and forces with the mouse, drop obstacles into a wind tunnel and watch a Kármán vortex street peel off behind them, set up a heavy fluid over a light one and watch the Rayleigh–Taylor instability grow its mushroom fingers. Render modes switch between dye, velocity field, vorticity (local spin, rendered as glowing filaments), and pressure. In plain terms: the same equations that govern weather, aircraft wings and cream stirred into coffee, running live in a browser tab — and unlike the many toy demos online, this one is checked against published benchmark solutions.

The science

Incompressible flow obeys the Navier–Stokes equations — Newton's second law written for a continuum, plus the constraint that the fluid doesn't compress:

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p + \nu \nabla^2 \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{f}, \qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} = 0$$

Each term has a physical job: \((\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}\) is advection — the fluid carrying its own momentum around, and the source of all the interesting nonlinearity; \(\nabla p\) is the pressure force that enforces incompressibility; \(\nu\nabla^2\mathbf{u}\) is viscous diffusion smearing out sharp features; \(\mathbf{f}\) is you, dragging the mouse. The single dimensionless number Reynolds number \(Re = UL/\nu\) controls the regime: low \(Re\) flows are syrupy and reversible, high \(Re\) flows shed vortices and cascade toward turbulence. (Whether smooth solutions always exist in 3D is a Clay Millennium Problem — a nice footnote for the explainer overlay.)

The solver uses the classic operator-splitting scheme (Chorin projection): advect, diffuse, add forces, then project the velocity field back onto the divergence-free space by solving a Poisson equation for pressure:

$$\nabla^2 p = \frac{\rho}{\Delta t}\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}^{*}, \qquad \mathbf{u}^{n+1} = \mathbf{u}^{*} - \frac{\Delta t}{\rho}\nabla p$$

This works because of the Helmholtz–Hodge decomposition: any vector field splits uniquely into a divergence-free part plus a gradient; the pressure solve peels the gradient part off. The industrial upgrade over toy demos is the advection scheme: plain semi-Lagrangian advection (Stam's "Stable Fluids") is unconditionally stable but numerically diffusive — it blurs the very vortices you want to see. This project implements MacCormack/BFECC advection: advect forward, advect back, measure the error, correct — second-order accurate, dramatically crisper filaments — plus vorticity confinement to re-inject the fine-scale swirl the grid cannot hold, controlled by \(\mathbf{f}_{vc} = \varepsilon\, (\hat{\mathbf{N}} \times \boldsymbol{\omega})\) where \(\boldsymbol{\omega} = \nabla\times\mathbf{u}\).

The computation

  • Every stage is a WebGPU compute shader over staggered (MAC) grid textures — pressure at cell centers, velocity components on faces, which prevents the checkerboard null-mode that plagues naive collocated grids.
  • The Poisson solve uses red–black Gauss–Seidel or a multigrid V-cycle on the GPU — the single biggest cost per frame, and the knob between speed and incompressibility error.
  • Validation suite: the lid-driven cavity benchmark at \(Re = 100\) and \(Re = 1000\) against the classic Ghia et al. (1982) reference velocity profiles, and vortex-shedding frequency behind a cylinder checked against the empirical Strouhal relation \(St \approx 0.198(1 - 19.7/Re)\). Failing benchmarks fail CI.
  • Scenario presets: wind tunnel with drag/lift readouts, Rayleigh–Taylor, buoyant smoke plume, double-shear-layer instability; screenshot and video export.

4 · Options Risk Topography

The implied-volatility surface as living terrain — and the models that try to explain it.

Derivatives Pricing Stochastic Calculus · Optimization WebGL Surfaces

What it is

An options analytics platform rendered as landscape: the implied-volatility surface of an underlying (from historical chain data) drawn as animated 3D terrain over strike and expiry, its smile and skew visible as valleys and ridges; the Greeks of any position drawn as flow-fields over the same terrain; the P&L of arbitrary multi-leg strategies (condors, butterflies, calendars) as topography you can walk through time. Underneath, real model calibration: fit Heston and SABR to the observed surface and render the residuals as a heatmap — literally seeing where the model fails. In plain terms: an option's market price encodes the market's forecast of future turbulence; collect thousands of options and you get a whole surface of forecasts, and its shape — why out-of-the-money puts cost "too much" — is one of the great stories in finance, told here visually.

The science

The foundation is Black–Scholes: under a lognormal stock model, the no-arbitrage price of a European call satisfies the PDE

$$\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + rS\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - rV = 0$$

with closed-form solution \(C = S\,N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)\), where \(d_{1,2} = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r \pm \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}\).

Implied volatility inverts this: given a market price, find the \(\sigma\) that reproduces it (a 1-D root-find via Newton or rational approximation, done thousands of times per surface). If Black–Scholes were true, the surface would be flat. It isn't — it smiles and skews, steeply for equity puts (the market's memory of 1987) — and that structure is the object of study. Key mathematical layers:

  • The Greeks — sensitivities \(\Delta = \partial V/\partial S\), \(\Gamma = \partial^2 V/\partial S^2\), vega \(= \partial V/\partial\sigma\), \(\Theta = \partial V/\partial t\) — the risk coordinates every desk lives in, here rendered as vector and scalar fields over the terrain.
  • Heston model — variance itself follows a mean-reverting square-root process: $$dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sqrt{v_t}\,S_t\,dW_t^S, \qquad dv_t = \kappa(\bar{v} - v_t)\,dt + \xi\sqrt{v_t}\,dW_t^v, \qquad d\langle W^S, W^v\rangle_t = \rho\,dt$$ The negative correlation \(\rho < 0\) generates the skew; pricing goes through the characteristic function and a numerically delicate complex-plane integration (the Heston "little trap" formulation).
  • SABR — the desk-standard smile model, with the Hagan asymptotic formula giving implied vol in closed form from parameters \((\alpha, \beta, \rho, \nu)\); fast enough to calibrate per-expiry in milliseconds.
  • Calibration is a genuinely hard nonlinear least-squares problem: $$\min_{\theta}\; \sum_{i} w_i \left(\sigma^{\text{model}}_i(\theta) - \sigma^{\text{mkt}}_i\right)^2$$ ill-conditioned, multi-modal, parameter-degenerate — solved with Levenberg–Marquardt plus multi-start, with the Feller condition \(2\kappa\bar{v} \ge \xi^2\) surfaced as a visible constraint.
  • No-arbitrage geometry — a valid surface can't have butterfly arbitrage (convexity in strike: \(\partial^2 C/\partial K^2 \ge 0\), which is, beautifully, the risk-neutral density by Breeden–Litzenberger) or calendar arbitrage (total variance \(\sigma^2 T\) non-decreasing in \(T\)). Violations in raw data are detected and flagged on the terrain.

The computation

  • Vectorized pricing/implied-vol engine (thousands of contracts per frame), Hagan-SABR per expiry, Heston via characteristic-function integration with Gauss–Laguerre quadrature.
  • Surface construction with arbitrage-aware smoothing (SVI parameterization per slice as an option); residual heatmaps of model-vs-market rendered directly on the 3D terrain.
  • Strategy builder: compose legs, see position Greeks aggregate live, scrub time-to-expiry and spot to watch the P&L topography deform.
  • Test suite: put–call parity, Black–Scholes closed-form vs Monte Carlo cross-checks, Heston pricer vs published reference values, calibration recovery tests (synthesize a surface from known parameters, verify the fitter recovers them).

5 · N-Body Galaxy Collider

Gravity, a million times over: colliding galaxies computed and rendered in real time.

Gravitational Dynamics Numerical Integration · Tree Algorithms WebGPU · HDR Rendering

What it is

A gravitational N-body simulator pushing a million-plus particles: set two spiral galaxies on a collision course and watch tidal tails unwind over hundreds of millions of simulated years — the Antennae galaxies, or the Milky Way–Andromeda merger due in ~4.5 billion years — rendered with HDR bloom so dense star regions genuinely glow, with time-scrubbing to run the collision backwards and forwards. In plain terms: every star pulls on every other star; with \(N\) stars that's \(N^2\) pulls per step — a million particles would need a trillion force calculations per frame, which is impossible — so the whole project is about the beautiful approximations that make it possible anyway.

The science

The system is pure Newton:

$$\ddot{\mathbf{r}}_i = -G\sum_{j\neq i} m_j \frac{\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j} {\left(\lVert\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j\rVert^2 + \epsilon^2\right)^{3/2}}$$

The \(\epsilon\) is the softening length — it caps the force between very close particles. It's not a hack: each particle represents thousands of real stars, and softening encodes that a star cluster isn't a point mass. Too small and close encounters explode the integrator; too large and structure blurs. The docs treat this tradeoff honestly, with the standard rule-of-thumb scaling \(\epsilon \sim R\, N^{-1/3}\).

Three pieces of serious math make it work:

  • Barnes–Hut tree algorithm. Recursively divide space into an octree; for a distant cluster of stars, use its center of mass instead of every member. A cell of size \(s\) at distance \(d\) is "distant enough" when \(s/d < \theta\) (opening angle, typically \(\theta \approx 0.5\)). Cost drops from \(O(N^2)\) to \(O(N \log N)\) — the difference between impossible and interactive. (The GPU-friendly alternative — all-pairs in shared-memory tiles — is exact and brutally parallel; the project benchmarks both.)
  • Symplectic integration. Ordinary integrators (Euler, RK4) slowly leak or gain energy, and over thousands of orbital periods a galaxy artificially evaporates or collapses. The leapfrog / kick–drift–kick scheme $$\mathbf{v}_{1/2} = \mathbf{v}_0 + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbf{a}_0, \qquad \mathbf{r}_1 = \mathbf{r}_0 + \Delta t\,\mathbf{v}_{1/2}, \qquad \mathbf{v}_1 = \mathbf{v}_{1/2} + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbf{a}_1$$ is symplectic: it exactly conserves a slightly-perturbed energy, so the true energy oscillates in a bounded band forever instead of drifting. Same cost as naive integration; vastly better physics. A live energy-drift monitor \(|\Delta E / E_0|\) is rendered on screen as the honesty meter.
  • Galaxy initial conditions. You can't scatter stars randomly and call it a galaxy — it must start in dynamical equilibrium or it instantly rings and relaxes into the wrong shape. Disks are initialized with exponential surface density \(\Sigma(R) = \Sigma_0 e^{-R/R_d}\), circular velocities from the enclosed mass (including a dark-matter halo term — this is exactly the flat-rotation-curve evidence for dark matter), and velocity dispersion set by the Toomre stability criterion $$Q = \frac{\sigma_R\,\kappa}{3.36\,G\,\Sigma} \gtrsim 1$$ so the disk neither shatters into clumps (\(Q < 1\)) nor becomes too hot to form spiral structure.

The payoff physics: tidal tails (the long streamers in every merger photo) emerge naturally from differential gravity during close passage; dynamical friction gradually sinks the two cores into a single merged elliptical — you watch morphology transform, the actual mechanism by which the universe builds elliptical galaxies.

The computation

  • WebGPU compute pipeline: tiled all-pairs kernel for exactness at \(N \le 10^5\); Barnes–Hut octree (built on CPU or GPU) for \(N \ge 10^6\); fixed-timestep leapfrog with per-preset \(\Delta t\).
  • HDR rendering: additive-blended point sprites → bloom (bright-pass + Gaussian pyramid) → filmic tonemapping; color by stellar population or by local velocity for a shockwave view.
  • Presets: Antennae (NGC 4038/39), MW–Andromeda, retrograde vs prograde encounters (dramatically different tail structure — a genuine 1972 Toomre & Toomre result), plummer-sphere cluster collapse.
  • Tests: two-body circular orbit closes on itself to tolerance; energy drift bounded over \(10^4\) steps; momentum conserved to floating-point tolerance; Toomre-\(Q\) verified on generated initial conditions.
Common engineering spine. All five share the same production skeleton: browser-native (WebGL2 or WebGPU, no install), deployable as static sites to GitHub Pages, each with a physics/math explainer overlay, preset scenario tours, screenshot/video export, and — the industrial-strength part — a validation suite asserting the science (conserved quantities, benchmark solutions, stylized facts, no-arbitrage constraints) rather than just "it renders".
