0. What is the GPU actually computing?

Open the lab, drag the mouse through the wind tunnel, and vortices peel off the cylinder in an alternating street. Switch presets and heavy fluid crashes through light fluid in mushroom-shaped fingers, or a smoke plume buckles and rolls. Five demos, five behaviors — and one pair of equations behind all of them, solved thousands of times per second in WebGPU compute shaders:

$$\underbrace{\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t}}_{\text{local change}} \;+\;\underbrace{(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\,\mathbf{u}}_{\text{self-transport}} \;=\;\underbrace{-\nabla p}_{\text{pressure push}} \;+\;\underbrace{\nu\,\nabla^2 \mathbf{u}}_{\text{friction}} \;+\;\underbrace{\mathbf{f}}_{\text{body force}}, \qquad\quad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0.$$

Here $\mathbf{u}$ is the fluid’s velocity field (a little arrow at every point — defined carefully in §1), $t$ is time, $p$ is pressure, $\nu$ (Greek “nu”) is the viscosity — the fluid’s internal friction — and $\mathbf{f}$ is any outside push per unit of fluid, such as buoyancy or your mouse drag. The symbol $\nabla$ (“del” or “nabla”) is a differentiation machine we rebuild from scratch in §2. Fig. 1 lays the same equation out as a map of this page.

The plan is one straight line. §1 pins down what a “field” is. §2 rebuilds the four derivative operations ($\nabla$-things) the equations use, each with a picture and a proof. §3 explains the strange-looking left-hand side: it is nothing but acceleration, written for a moving drop of fluid. §4 derives $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0$ from “fluid is neither created nor squashed.” §5 assembles the right-hand side from Newton’s second law. §6 reveals pressure’s real job — it is not a given force but the enforcer of §4 — which is exactly how the GPU solver treats it. §7 compresses all parameters into one number, the Reynolds number. §8 walks one solver time step, and §9 specializes the equations to each of the five demos. §10 is the honest ledger of what this model ignores.

1. Fields: the objects the equations govern

Everything in Fig. 1 is a field: a quantity with a value at every point of the domain, at every moment. That is the first mental shift from particle mechanics — the unknown is not a position, it is a whole spatial pattern evolving in time.

Definition 1.1 (field). A scalar field assigns a number to each point: $p(x,y,t)$. A vector field assigns an arrow: $\mathbf{u}(x,y,t) = \big(u(x,y,t),\, v(x,y,t)\big)$, where $u$ is the horizontal component and $v$ the vertical one. Position is $\mathbf{x}=(x,y)$; the lab is two-dimensional, so there are two coordinates. Note the typography: bold $\mathbf{u}$ is the velocity vector; italic $u$ is its $x$-component.

A partial derivative $\partial p/\partial x$ is the ordinary one-variable derivative taken while every other variable is frozen: stand at a fixed $y$ and fixed time, walk in $x$, and record the slope. That is the only calculus prerequisite; everything else is assembled from it.

The lab evolves exactly four fields, and you can display each one from the View menu:

FieldSymbolTypeIn the lab

Pressure$p$scalar“Pressure” view — computed fresh each step (§6)
Dye / buoyancy scalar$s$scalar“Dye” view — the smoke you inject; in two demos it also drives the flow (§9)

The dye obeys the simplest transport law, worth meeting now because every harder equation on this page contains it: $\partial s/\partial t + (\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)s = 0$, read “$s$ is carried by the flow and otherwise unchanged.” What that operator $(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)$ does is the business of the next two sections.

2. The del operator ∇: four ways to differentiate a field

With fields defined (§1), we need their derivatives. The symbol $\nabla$ packages the partial derivatives into a formal vector, $\nabla = \left(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x},\, \tfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\right)$, and every $\nabla$-expression in Fig. 1 is one of four combinations of it. Each gets a definition, a picture, and a proof of the geometric meaning the picture claims.

2.1 Gradient: which way is uphill

Definition 2.1 (gradient). For a scalar field $T(x,y)$, the gradient is the vector field $\nabla T = \left(\dfrac{\partial T}{\partial x},\, \dfrac{\partial T}{\partial y}\right).$
Proposition 2.2 (the gradient points uphill, as fast as possible). Let $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ be any unit vector (an arrow of length 1). The rate of change of $T$ walking in direction $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ is $\nabla T \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}$, and it is largest when $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ points along $\nabla T$; that largest rate is $|\nabla T|$.
Walk from $\mathbf{x}$ with arc length $\sigma$: the path is $\mathbf{x}+\sigma\hat{\mathbf{n}} =(x+\sigma n_1,\; y+\sigma n_2)$. By the one-variable chain rule applied to $g(\sigma)=T(x+\sigma n_1,\, y+\sigma n_2)$, $$\frac{dg}{d\sigma} = \frac{\partial T}{\partial x}\,n_1 + \frac{\partial T}{\partial y}\,n_2 = \nabla T\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}} = |\nabla T|\,\cos\theta,$$ where $\theta$ is the angle between $\nabla T$ and $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ (that is the geometric definition of the dot product). Since $\cos\theta \le 1$ with equality exactly when $\theta=0$, the walk gains $T$ fastest when it follows $\nabla T$, at rate $|\nabla T|$. ∎

In the momentum equation the gradient appears with a minus sign, $-\nabla p$: the pressure force points downhill, from high pressure toward low. Fig. 2 already contains that story — flip every green arrow.

2.2 Divergence: is this point a source or a drain?

Definition 2.3 (divergence). For a vector field $\mathbf{u}=(u,v)$, the divergence is the scalar field $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} = \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \dfrac{\partial v}{\partial y}.$
Proposition 2.4 (divergence = net outflow rate per unit area). Interpret $\mathbf{u}$ as a velocity (area of fluid moved per unit time crossing a unit of boundary length). For the small rectangle $[x,\,x+\delta x]\times[y,\,y+\delta y]$, the net rate $Q$ at which fluid area leaves through the boundary satisfies $$\frac{Q}{\delta x\,\delta y} \;\longrightarrow\; \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} \qquad\text{as } \delta x,\delta y \to 0.$$
Fluid crosses the right edge (at $x+\delta x$, length $\delta y$) at rate $\int_y^{y+\delta y} u(x+\delta x, y')\,dy'$, outward because positive $u$ points out of the box there. On the left edge positive $u$ points into the box, so its outward rate carries a minus sign. Top and bottom work the same way with $v$. Summing the four edges, $$Q=\int_y^{y+\delta y}\!\big[u(x+\delta x,y')-u(x,y')\big]dy' +\int_x^{x+\delta x}\!\big[v(x',y+\delta y)-v(x',y)\big]dx'.$$ Taylor’s theorem gives $u(x+\delta x,y')-u(x,y') = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}(x,y')\, \delta x + O(\delta x^2)$, and on the short edge the integrand is constant up to $O(\delta y)$, so the first integral is $\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\,\delta x\,\delta y$ plus terms smaller by a factor of $\delta$. The second integral likewise yields $\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}\,\delta x\,\delta y$. Dividing by the area $\delta x\,\delta y$ and shrinking the box kills the correction terms, leaving $\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}$. ∎

Proposition 2.4 is why $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0$ will express “fluid does not pile up or thin out” in §4 — and the right panel of Fig. 3 is a preview that this constraint still permits arbitrarily interesting swirling motion.

2.3 Curl: how fast does the fluid locally spin?

Definition 2.5 (vorticity / 2-D curl). The vorticity of $\mathbf{u}=(u,v)$ is the scalar field $\omega = \dfrac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y}.$ (In three dimensions the curl is a vector; in a 2-D flow only its out-of-plane component survives, and $\omega$ is that component.)
Proposition 2.6 (vorticity = twice the local spin rate). Place a tiny cross of two fluid fibers at a point, one along $x$ and one along $y$, and let the flow carry their endpoints. The average of the two fibers’ counter-clockwise rotation rates is $\omega/2$. In particular, for rigid rotation at angular velocity $\Omega$, $\;\omega = 2\Omega$.
Fiber along $x$, of tiny length $\delta$: its far end moves vertically faster than its near end by $v(x+\delta,y)-v(x,y) \approx \frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\,\delta$. A relative vertical speed $\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\delta$ at lever arm $\delta$ turns the fiber counter-clockwise at angular rate $\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}$ (radians per time; positive = counter-clockwise with $y$ up). Fiber along $y$: its far end moves horizontally faster by $\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\,\delta$; a $+x$ drift at the top of a vertical fiber is a clockwise turn, rate $-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}$ counter-clockwise. Averaging: $$\tfrac12\!\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right) = \frac{\omega}{2}.$$ For rigid rotation $\mathbf{u}=\Omega\,(-(y-y_0),\,(x-x_0))$: $\;\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} =\Omega$ and $\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}=-\Omega$, so $\omega = \Omega-(-\Omega)=2\Omega$. ∎

Vorticity never appears in the equations of Fig. 1 explicitly, but it is the quantity CFD people actually look at, because pressure forces cannot create it — only walls, buoyancy, and viscosity can. The lab’s vorticity view computes Definition 2.5 on the GPU (the VORT kernel in src/shaders.js).

2.4 Laplacian: how far below your neighbours’ average are you?

Definition 2.7 (Laplacian). For a scalar field $f$, the Laplacian is $\nabla^2 f = \nabla\cdot(\nabla f) = \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} + \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2}.$ For a vector field it acts on each component: $\nabla^2\mathbf{u} = (\nabla^2 u,\, \nabla^2 v)$.
Proposition 2.8 (Laplacian = neighbourhood-average excess). Let $\bar f_\delta(x,y)$ be the average of $f$ over the four points a distance $\delta$ away along the axes: $\bar f_\delta = \tfrac14\big[f(x{+}\delta,y)+f(x{-}\delta,y)+f(x,y{+}\delta)+ f(x,y{-}\delta)\big]$. Then $$\bar f_\delta - f = \frac{\delta^2}{4}\,\nabla^2 f + O(\delta^4).$$ So $\nabla^2 f \gt 0$ means the point sits below the average of its neighbours, and $\nabla^2 f \lt 0$ means above.
Taylor expand each neighbour to fourth order. In $x$: $f(x\pm\delta,y) = f \pm \delta\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + \frac{\delta^2}{2}\, \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} \pm \frac{\delta^3}{6}\,\frac{\partial^3 f}{\partial x^3} + O(\delta^4)$. Adding the $\pm$ pair cancels every odd-order term: $f(x{+}\delta,y)+f(x{-}\delta,y) = 2f + \delta^2\,\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}+O(\delta^4)$, and the $y$ pair likewise gives $2f + \delta^2\,\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2}+O(\delta^4)$. Summing all four and dividing by 4: $\bar f_\delta = f + \frac{\delta^2}{4}\big(\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}+ \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2}\big) + O(\delta^4)$. Subtract $f$. ∎

This is why $\nu\nabla^2\mathbf{u}$ means friction: wherever a velocity component spikes above its surroundings, the term is negative and drags it back toward the neighbourhood average — momentum leaks from fast fluid to slow. It is also, verbatim, the discrete formula in the lab’s diffusion kernel: c + nu*dt*(l+r+b+t-4.0*c) in src/shaders.js is $f + \nu\,\Delta t\cdot 4(\bar f_\delta - f)/\delta^2$ with grid spacing $\delta = 1$.

3. Following the fluid: the material derivative

The operators of §2 differentiate in space. The left side of the momentum equation is about differentiating in time — and there are two inequivalent ways to do it, whose difference generates the strangest-looking term in Fig. 1.

Definition 3.1 (parcel; the two time derivatives). A parcel is an idealized small blob of fluid that moves with the flow: its path $\mathbf{X}(t)$ obeys $\dot{\mathbf{X}}(t) = \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{X}(t),\,t)$. The local derivative $\partial\mathbf{u}/\partial t$ measures change at a point fixed in space (a lab thermometer bolted to the wall). The material derivative, written $D\mathbf{u}/Dt$, measures change experienced by the moving parcel (a thermometer drifting with the water).

Newton’s second law applies to matter, so it needs the parcel’s acceleration — the material derivative. The whole content of the theorem below is a chain rule.

Theorem 3.2 (material derivative). For a differentiable velocity field, the acceleration of the parcel passing through $\mathbf{x}$ at time $t$ is $$\boxed{\;\frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} \;=\; \frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} \;+\; (\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\,\mathbf{u}\;}\tag{3.1}$$ where $(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla) = u\frac{\partial}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial}{\partial y}$ is applied to each component of $\mathbf{u}$.
Take the $x$-component; the $y$-component is identical with $v$ in place of $u$. The parcel’s horizontal velocity at time $t$ is the field sampled along its own path: $u\big(X(t),\,Y(t),\,t\big)$, where $\mathbf{X}=(X,Y)$. Differentiate with the multivariable chain rule — one term per argument: $$\frac{d}{dt}\,u\big(X(t),Y(t),t\big) = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\,\dot X + \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\,\dot Y + \frac{\partial u}{\partial t}.$$ By Definition 3.1 the parcel moves with the fluid: $\dot X = u$ and $\dot Y = v$ (evaluated at the parcel’s position). Substituting, $$\frac{Du}{Dt} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)u.$$ Stacking the two components gives (3.1). ∎

The term $(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}$ is called advection (transport by the flow) of velocity by itself, and it is worth staring at the case where it is the only source of acceleration. Fig. 6 shows a steady nozzle: the field never changes ($\partial\mathbf{u}/\partial t = 0$ everywhere), yet the parcel visibly speeds up as it rides into the narrow section, because it is carried into a region where the field is faster. Along the centerline (3.1) reduces to $Du/Dt = u\,\partial u/\partial x$: speed times spatial speedup.

This term is also the troublemaker: it is nonlinear — the unknown $\mathbf{u}$ multiplies its own derivative. Every rich behavior in the lab (vortex streets, mushroom fingers, billow roll-ups) and every open mathematical question about these equations traces back to this one quadratic term. A linear equation cannot produce a Kármán street.

4. No fluid appears or vanishes: ∇·u = 0

The momentum equation will come from Newton’s law in §5; first we need the bookkeeping law it must obey. With divergence understood as net outflow per area (Proposition 2.4), conservation of mass is one short argument away.

Theorem 4.1 (incompressibility). Let the fluid have constant, uniform density $\rho$ (mass per area in 2-D), and suppose mass is neither created nor destroyed. Then $$\boxed{\;\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} = 0\;}\tag{4.1}$$ at every point and every time.
Fix a small box of area $\delta x\,\delta y$. The mass inside is $\rho\,\delta x\,\delta y$, and it is constant in time because $\rho$ is a constant and the box does not move. Mass changes only by transport through the boundary (no creation or destruction), so the net mass outflow rate must vanish. Net mass outflow rate is $\rho$ times net area outflow rate $Q$, hence $Q = 0$ for every small box. By Proposition 2.4, $Q = (\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} + \varepsilon)\, \delta x\,\delta y$ where $\varepsilon\to0$ as the box shrinks; if $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}$ were nonzero at some point, a small enough box around that point would have $Q\neq0$ — a contradiction. ∎

“Constant density” sounds wrong for air, which is compressible. The honest statement: treating a gas as incompressible is accurate when flow speeds are far below the speed of sound (low Mach number — the flow-speed-to-sound-speed ratio), because then pressure equalizes so fast that density differences never build up. The cost is that sound waves are removed from the model entirely — pressure becomes instantaneous action-at-a-distance (§6, §10).

5. Newton’s second law for a blob of fluid: the momentum equation

Now the physics. Take a parcel occupying a small box of area $\delta V = \delta x\,\delta y$ and apply $m\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{F}$. Its mass is $\rho\,\delta V$ (with $\rho$ constant by §4); its acceleration is the material derivative of §3. What remains is to inventory the forces: $$\rho\,\delta V\,\frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} \;=\; \mathbf{F}_{\text{pressure}} + \mathbf{F}_{\text{viscous}} + \mathbf{F}_{\text{body}}.$$ We compute each of the three, per unit volume, and then assemble.

5.1 The pressure force is −∇p

Definition 5.1 (pressure). Within a fluid at rest — and, to leading order, within a moving one — each side of any small surface pushes on the other side, perpendicular to the surface, with the same strength in every orientation. Pressure $p(x,y,t)$ is that push per unit of surface length (per unit area in 3-D). It is a scalar field: one number per point, no direction of its own.
Proposition 5.2 (net pressure force per volume). The surrounding fluid pushes on a small box of fluid with net force $$\mathbf{F}_{\text{pressure}} = -\nabla p\;\delta V + O(\delta^3).\tag{5.1}$$
By Definition 5.1 the fluid left of the box pushes it in the $+x$ direction with force $p(x,y_c)\,\delta y + O(\delta^3)$ (pressure evaluated mid-face at height $y_c$; the variation of $p$ along the short face contributes only higher order). The fluid on the right pushes in $-x$ with $p(x+\delta x, y_c)\,\delta y$. The top and bottom faces push only vertically (pressure is perpendicular to each surface) so they contribute nothing horizontal. Net $x$-force: $$\big[p(x,y_c) - p(x+\delta x,y_c)\big]\delta y = -\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}\,\delta x\,\delta y + O(\delta^3),$$ by Taylor’s theorem. The identical argument on top/bottom faces gives the $y$-component $-\frac{\partial p}{\partial y}\,\delta x\,\delta y$. Stack the components: $-\nabla p\,\delta V$. ∎

Two consequences worth naming. Only pressure differences exert force — adding a constant to $p$ everywhere changes nothing, which is why the lab may pin $p=0$ at the outflow edge without loss. And in the lab’s pressure view you can see (5.1) at work: high pressure (red) where flow rams the cylinder’s nose, low (blue) in vortex cores — the front-to-back difference integrated over the cylinder surface is precisely the drag readout.

5.2 The viscous force is μ∇²u

Definition 5.3 (Newtonian fluid). Adjacent layers of moving fluid drag each other: a surface inside the fluid transmits, besides pressure, a shear stress (force per unit length, along the surface). A fluid is Newtonian if this stress is proportional to the local rate of deformation, with stress tensor components $$\tau_{xx} = 2\mu\frac{\partial u}{\partial x},\qquad \tau_{yy} = 2\mu\frac{\partial v}{\partial y},\qquad \tau_{xy} = \tau_{yx} = \mu\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\right),$$ where the constant $\mu$ is the dynamic viscosity and $\tau_{ij}$ is the $i$-directed force per unit length transmitted across a surface whose outward normal points along $j$. This is a constitutive law — an experimental fact about water and air, stated rather than derived, like Hooke’s law for springs.
Proposition 5.4 (net viscous force per volume). For a Newtonian fluid satisfying $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0$, $$\mathbf{F}_{\text{viscous}} = \mu\,\nabla^2\mathbf{u}\;\delta V + O(\delta^3).\tag{5.2}$$
Consider the $x$-component of force on the box. Across each face, Definition 5.3 gives the transmitted $x$-force per unit length: $\tau_{xx}$ on the vertical faces, $\tau_{xy}$ on the horizontal ones, each pulling with opposite orientation on opposite faces. Exactly as in the proof of Proposition 5.2, each opposite-face pair survives only through its derivative: $$\frac{F_x}{\delta V} = \frac{\partial \tau_{xx}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial \tau_{xy}}{\partial y} + O(\delta) = \mu\left(2\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 v}{\partial x\,\partial y}\right).$$ Regroup the right-hand side by splitting $2\,\partial^2 u/\partial x^2$ and factoring an $x$-derivative out of the leftovers: $$\frac{F_x}{\delta V} = \mu\left(\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2}\right) + \mu\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y}\right) = \mu\,\nabla^2 u + \mu\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}).$$ The last term is zero by Theorem 4.1. The $y$-component is the mirror-image computation, ending in $\mu\nabla^2 v + \mu\,\partial_y(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u})$. Stacking gives (5.2). ∎

Read (5.2) through Proposition 2.8: viscosity accelerates a parcel toward the average velocity of its neighbours. That is friction in field form — it flattens velocity spikes, widens shear layers, and converts kinetic energy to heat (the heat is not tracked in this model).

5.3 Body forces: gravity, buoyancy, and your mouse

The third force needs no derivation: an externally imposed force acting throughout the volume — gravity, or the buoyancy of hot fluid — contributes $\rho\,\mathbf{g}\,\delta V$ for force $\mathbf{g}$ per unit mass. In the lab this term is literal user input as well: dragging the mouse runs the FORCES kernel, which adds a Gaussian-shaped velocity kick centered on the cursor — an $\mathbf{f}$ patch you steer by hand. The buoyancy used by two demos (§9) is the Boussinesq approximation: density differences are ignored everywhere except in the gravity term, where they become a force $B\,s\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ proportional to the transported scalar $s$; the uniform part of gravity is dropped because it is exactly cancelled by a hydrostatic (depth-proportional) pressure field and moves nothing.

5.4 Assembling the equation

Theorem 5.5 (incompressible Navier–Stokes). A constant-density Newtonian fluid under body force $\mathbf{g}$ per unit mass obeys $$\rho\,\frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} = -\nabla p + \mu\nabla^2\mathbf{u} + \rho\,\mathbf{g}, \qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0.\tag{5.3}$$ Dividing by $\rho$ and writing $\nu = \mu/\rho$ (kinematic viscosity), $\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{g}$, and $p$ for $p/\rho$ (“kinematic pressure,” the convention the solver uses): $$\boxed{\;\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\,\mathbf{u} = -\nabla p + \nu\,\nabla^2\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{f}, \qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0.\;}\tag{5.4}$$
Newton’s second law for the parcel: $\rho\,\delta V\,\frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} = \mathbf{F}_{\text{pressure}} + \mathbf{F}_{\text{viscous}} + \mathbf{F}_{\text{body}}$. Substitute Proposition 5.2, Proposition 5.4, and $\rho\mathbf{g}\,\delta V$, divide by $\delta V$, and let the box shrink so every $O(\delta)$ correction vanishes; this yields (5.3), with (4.1) carried along from Theorem 4.1. Divide (5.3) by the constant $\rho$ and expand $D\mathbf{u}/Dt$ with Theorem 3.2 to get (5.4). Renaming $p/\rho \mapsto p$ is harmless because only $\nabla p$ ever acts, and rescaling by a constant merely rescales the field that the pressure solver computes anyway (§6). ∎

Count unknowns against equations: three unknown fields in 2-D ($u$, $v$, $p$) and three equations (two momentum components plus the constraint). The bookkeeping balances — but notice that no equation begins “$\partial p/\partial t = \ldots$”. Pressure has no evolution law of its own, which is the puzzle the next section resolves.

6. What pressure is for: the Poisson equation and projection

In (5.4), $\mathbf{u}$ has a time derivative but $p$ does not. Pressure is not a substance with its own dynamics; it is a constraint force — the field that takes whatever value is needed, instant by instant, to keep $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0$ true. This is the same logic as the tension in an inextensible string: no law prescribes the tension directly, it is whatever the constraint demands. Demanding consistency pins $p$ down completely.

Proposition 6.1 (pressure Poisson equation). If $\mathbf{u}$ solves (5.4) and stays divergence-free, then at each instant the pressure satisfies $$\nabla^2 p = \nabla\cdot\big(\mathbf{f} - (\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}\big).\tag{6.1}$$
Apply the divergence operator to every term of (5.4), using that $\nabla\cdot$ commutes with $\partial/\partial t$ and with $\nabla^2$ (partial derivatives commute for smooth fields): $$\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}) + \nabla\cdot\big[(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}\big] = -\nabla\cdot(\nabla p) + \nu\,\nabla^2(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}) + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{f}.$$ The constraint $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0$ holds at all times, so the first and fourth terms vanish. By Definition 2.7, $\nabla\cdot(\nabla p) = \nabla^2 p$. Rearranging leaves (6.1). ∎

Equation (6.1) is a Poisson equation: given the right-hand side everywhere, it determines $p$ everywhere at once (up to an additive constant, which Proposition 5.2 already told us is physically invisible). No time derivative appears — change the flow in one corner and the pressure field adjusts globally at the same instant. That is the incompressible limit’s infinitely fast sound speed made explicit (§10).

The GPU solver uses this structure directly, in discrete form (Chorin’s projection method). Each step it first ignores the constraint, producing a provisional velocity $\mathbf{u}^*$ that has absorbed advection, viscosity, and forces but has nonzero divergence; then it solves a Poisson equation $\nabla^2\varphi = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}^*$ for a pressure-like field $\varphi$ and subtracts its gradient: $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}^* - \nabla\varphi$. The result is divergence-free — and it is the smallest possible correction to $\mathbf{u}^*$ with that property, in the precise least-squares sense proved in Appendix A. Fig. 10 shows the operation on a real computed field.

The lab exposes this machinery: the Pressure iters slider sets how many relaxation sweeps the discrete Poisson solve gets per step. Starve it (slide to 10) and the constraint is enforced only approximately — dye visibly bunches and smears. The validation suite’s first test is exactly Fig. 10’s experiment on the GPU: one projection of a random field must cut its divergence by a factor above 100; the measured drop is 2531×.

7. One number picks the regime: the Reynolds number

Equation (5.4) seems to have several free parameters — the fluid’s viscosity, the domain’s size, the flow’s speed. Rescaling shows they collapse into a single knob, which is why one WebGPU solver can be honest about wind tunnels and lid-driven cavities alike.

Proposition 7.1 (nondimensionalization). Pick a characteristic length $L$ (say, an obstacle diameter) and speed $U$ (say, the inflow speed), and measure everything in those units: $\mathbf{x} = L\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$, $\mathbf{u} = U\tilde{\mathbf{u}}$, $t = (L/U)\,\tilde t$, $p = U^2 \tilde p$, $\mathbf{f} = (U^2/L)\,\tilde{\mathbf{f}}$. Then (5.4) becomes $$\frac{\partial \tilde{\mathbf{u}}}{\partial \tilde t} + (\tilde{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\tilde\nabla)\tilde{\mathbf{u}} = -\tilde\nabla \tilde p + \frac{1}{\mathrm{Re}}\tilde\nabla^2\tilde{\mathbf{u}} + \tilde{\mathbf{f}}, \qquad \boxed{\;\mathrm{Re} = \frac{UL}{\nu}\;}\tag{7.1}$$ — identical in form, with every parameter absorbed into the single Reynolds number $\mathrm{Re}$.
Each derivative rescales by the chain rule: $\frac{\partial}{\partial x} = \frac{1}{L}\frac{\partial}{\partial \tilde x}$ (so $\nabla = \tilde\nabla/L$, $\nabla^2 = \tilde\nabla^2/L^2$) and $\frac{\partial}{\partial t} = \frac{U}{L}\frac{\partial}{\partial \tilde t}$. Substitute into each term of (5.4): the local term becomes $\frac{U^2}{L}\partial\tilde{\mathbf{u}}/\partial\tilde t$; advection becomes $U\tilde{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\frac{\tilde\nabla}{L}(U\tilde{\mathbf{u}}) = \frac{U^2}{L}(\tilde{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\tilde\nabla)\tilde{\mathbf{u}}$; the pressure term $-\frac{\tilde\nabla}{L}(U^2\tilde p) = -\frac{U^2}{L}\tilde\nabla\tilde p$; viscosity $\nu\frac{\tilde\nabla^2}{L^2}U\tilde{\mathbf{u}} = \frac{\nu U}{L^2}\tilde\nabla^2 \tilde{\mathbf{u}}$; and the force $\frac{U^2}{L}\tilde{\mathbf{f}}$. Every term now carries $U^2/L$ except viscosity; divide through by $U^2/L$ and the viscous coefficient becomes $\frac{\nu U}{L^2}\cdot\frac{L}{U^2} = \frac{\nu}{UL} = \frac{1}{\mathrm{Re}}$. The constraint $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0$ rescales to $\tilde\nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{u}}=0$ trivially. ∎

Interpretation: $\mathrm{Re}$ is the strength of the nonlinear advection term relative to the viscous term — each term’s magnitude estimate is $U^2/L$ versus $\nu U/L^2$, and their ratio is $UL/\nu$. Small $\mathrm{Re}$: friction wins, flows are smooth, reversible-looking, and steady (honey). Large $\mathrm{Re}$: advection wins, flows fold and remix themselves (smoke, weather). The same geometry can sit on either side: the lab’s cylinder wake is steady below $\mathrm{Re}\approx47$ and sheds vortices above — a genuine qualitative transition controlled by one number.

Units in the lab: everything is measured in grid cells ($h=1$) and one lid/inflow speed ($U=1$), so setting a demo’s Reynolds number reduces to setting $\nu = UL/\mathrm{Re}$ in those units — e.g. the cavity runs $\nu = 256/1000$ on a $256^2$ grid for $\mathrm{Re}=1000$. That is Proposition 7.1 used as an engineering tool.

8. From equation to GPU: one time step of the solver

The equations are now assembled; here is how the lab advances them by one time step $\Delta t$, as encoded in Sim.encodeStep() (src/solver.js). The strategy is operator splitting: handle one term of (5.4) at a time, each as its own compute kernel, and let the projection of §6 repair the constraint last.

You can run stage 4 yourself in twenty lines of NumPy — this is Fig. 10’s experiment, and the printed numbers below are its actual output:

import numpy as np
n = 64
k = 2*np.pi*np.fft.fftfreq(n)
kx, ky = k[None, :], k[:, None]
k2 = kx**2 + ky**2
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
lp = np.exp(-2*k2)  # low-pass filter: keep the field smooth
u = np.real(np.fft.ifft2(lp*np.fft.fft2(rng.standard_normal((n, n)))))
v = np.real(np.fft.ifft2(lp*np.fft.fft2(rng.standard_normal((n, n)))))
div = np.real(np.fft.ifft2(1j*kx*np.fft.fft2(u) + 1j*ky*np.fft.fft2(v)))
k2[0, 0] = 1.0  # avoid 0/0; the mean of p is arbitrary anyway
p = np.fft.fft2(div)/(-k2)
p[0, 0] = 0.0
u -= np.real(np.fft.ifft2(1j*kx*p))
v -= np.real(np.fft.ifft2(1j*ky*p))
div2 = np.real(np.fft.ifft2(1j*kx*np.fft.fft2(u) + 1j*ky*np.fft.fft2(v)))
print("max |div| before:", float(np.abs(div).max()))
print("max |div| after: ", float(np.abs(div2).max()))

Output: max |div| before: 0.2859… and max |div| after: 1.902e-10. This uses a spectral (FFT) Poisson solve on a periodic domain, so the constraint lands at machine precision in one shot; the lab’s iterative Gauss–Seidel solve on a walled domain approaches the same answer sweep by sweep.

9.1 Wind tunnel: the Kármán vortex street

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u} = -\nabla p + \nu\nabla^2\mathbf{u}, \qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0 \qquad\text{— the full equation, driven by its boundaries.}$$

  • No body force. $\mathbf{f}$ is essentially zero; the flow is driven entirely by the boundary condition $\mathbf{u}=(U,0)$ at the inflow edge. (For its first 3000 steps the solver adds a tiny oscillating vertical force upstream — a numerical seed that breaks the perfect top–bottom symmetry, standing in for the imperfections real tunnels have for free.)
  • Viscosity is small but decisive. With $U=1$, cylinder diameter $D=32$ cells and $\nu=0.16$: $\;\mathrm{Re} = UD/\nu = 32/0.16 = 200$, comfortably above the shedding threshold $\mathrm{Re}\approx47$. Below the threshold, friction damps every wobble and the wake is a steady, symmetric pair of eddies; above it, the advection term amplifies wobbles into the street.
  • Check against the world. The shedding rhythm is measured by the Strouhal number $\mathrm{St} = fD/U$ ($f$ = shedding frequency). The validation suite runs this preset’s physics at $\mathrm{Re}=150$ and finds $\mathrm{St}=0.189$ against the empirical value $0.172$ from the relation $\mathrm{St}=0.198\,(1-19.7/\mathrm{Re})$ — 9.6% high, an offset explained by the walls being close enough to squeeze the wake (blockage).
  • What to watch. The dye stripes make folding visible; the vorticity view shows the street as alternating red/blue blobs — Proposition 2.6 painted in real time.

9.2 Rayleigh–Taylor: heavy over light

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u} = -\nabla p + B\,s\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}, \qquad \frac{\partial s}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)s = 0, \qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0, \qquad B \lt 0.$$

  • The scalar becomes a protagonist. Here $s$ is not decoration: the Boussinesq force $Bs\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ with $B=-0.012$ pulls fluid down in proportion to how much heavy dye it carries. Navier–Stokes becomes a coupled two-field system — $\mathbf{u}$ folds $s$, while $s$ forces $\mathbf{u}$.
  • No viscosity. $\nu=0$: nothing damps the fine structure, so the fingers’ edges sharpen and roll up until the grid scale intervenes. The secondary curls on each mushroom stem are Kelvin–Helmholtz billows (§9.4) riding on the shear between rising and sinking streams — one instability nesting inside another.
  • Growth you can time. Classical linear theory (Chandrasekhar 1961; stated here, not re-derived) gives a ripple of wavenumber $\kappa$ an initial exponential growth rate $\sigma=\sqrt{|B|\,\kappa/2}$ in Boussinesq variables. The seeded mode has $\kappa = 2\pi\cdot3/384 = 0.049$ per cell, so $\sigma = \sqrt{0.012\times0.049/2} \approx 0.017$ per time unit: the ripple’s amplitude should e-fold (grow by $2.7\times$) roughly every 58 steps — about half a second of wall-clock at the default 2 steps/frame and 60 fps, matching the visible onset before the growth turns nonlinear and mushroom-shaped.

▶ run Rayleigh–Taylor

9.3 Buoyant plume: a smoke source

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u} = -\nabla p + B\,s\,\hat{\mathbf{y}} + \varepsilon\,(\hat{\mathbf{N}}\times\boldsymbol{\omega}), \qquad \frac{\partial s}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)s = S(\mathbf{x}) - \lambda s, \qquad B \gt 0.$$

  • Same physics as Rayleigh–Taylor, opposite sign, different feeding. $B\gt0$ makes marked fluid rise. Instead of a one-shot initial condition there is a steady source $S(\mathbf{x})$ ($s$ is reset to 1 inside a 7-cell radius near the floor every step) plus a slow decay $\lambda$: the code multiplies $s$ by $0.9995$ each step, a half-life of $\ln 2/0.0005 \approx 1386$ steps ($\approx 12$ s of wall clock), so old smoke fades instead of filling the box. The result is a statistically steady churn rather than a single overturn.
  • One term here is confessed numerics, not physics. $\varepsilon(\hat{\mathbf{N}}\times \boldsymbol{\omega})$ is vorticity confinement: it detects where the grid has smeared small vortices ($\hat{\mathbf{N}}$ is the unit vector pointing up the gradient of $|\omega|$) and pushes energy back into the swirl, $\varepsilon=0.15$. Real Navier–Stokes has no such term; it compensates for resolution the grid does not have. Drag the Vorticity conf. slider to 0 to see the honest — blurrier — solution (§10).
  • Passive vs active, in one lab. Inject dye with the mouse far from the plume: it rides the flow but does not push it ($s$ enters $\mathbf{f}$ only where the buoyancy coefficient acts on it — and mouse dye and plume smoke share the same $s$ field here, so strictly your dye is buoyant too. Watch it start to rise.)

▶ run the plume

9.4 Double shear layer: Kelvin–Helmholtz roll-up

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u} = -\nabla p, \qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0 \qquad\text{— the Euler equation: no viscosity, no forcing.}$$

  • Nothing drives or damps this flow. With $\nu=0$ and $\mathbf{f}=0$, (5.4) reduces to Euler’s equation: all the energy is banked in the initial condition, and everything you see is the advection term rearranging it (pressure only enforces the constraint, as §6 showed).
  • Why a ripple must grow. Where the interface bows upward into the faster stream, the stream squeezes over the bump and speeds up locally; by the nozzle logic of Fig. 6 run in reverse — fast flow over a crest lowers pressure there (Bernoulli’s effect) — the crest gets sucked further up. The classical vortex-sheet result (Drazin & Reid; stated, not re-derived) makes it quantitative: a ripple of wavenumber $\kappa$ on a velocity jump $\Delta U$ grows like $e^{\sigma t}$ with $\sigma = \kappa\,\Delta U/2$.
  • Growth you can time. Here $\Delta U = 1$ (bands at $\pm\tfrac12$) and the seeded mode 4 has $\kappa = 2\pi\cdot4/384 = 0.065$, so $\sigma \approx 0.033$: e-folding every ∼31 time units, i.e. every ∼38 steps at $\Delta t = 0.8$ — a fraction of a second of wall clock, which is why the billows appear almost immediately.
  • Where you have seen it. This roll-up is the generic instability of any velocity jump: wind over water raising waves, billow clouds, the scalloped edges of Jupiter’s bands — and the secondary curls on Rayleigh–Taylor mushroom stems (§9.2).

▶ run the shear layer

9.5 Lid-driven cavity: the benchmark

$$(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u} = -\nabla p + \nu\nabla^2\mathbf{u}, \qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0 \qquad\text{— the steady state } (\partial\mathbf{u}/\partial t \to 0) \text{ the solver relaxes into.}$$

  • Viscosity is the protagonist. The lid imparts momentum only through the no-slip condition and the viscous term — switch $\nu$ off and the lid would slide over frictionless fluid, moving nothing. With $U=1$, $L=256$, $\nu=256/1000=0.256$: $\mathrm{Re}=UL/\nu=1000$ exactly, by the recipe of §7. The wall boundary layer thickness scales as $L/\sqrt{\mathrm{Re}} \approx 8$ cells, which is why this preset runs a finer effective resolution than it looks.
  • Time-marched to equilibrium. The solver always integrates the unsteady equation (5.4); this preset simply converges — the left side’s $\partial\mathbf{u}/\partial t$ decays toward zero and what remains is the boxed steady balance of advection, pressure, and friction.
  • Check against published data. This exact configuration is the standard CFD benchmark of Ghia, Ghia & Shin (1982), who tabulated the velocity profiles along the cavity’s centerlines. The validation suite compares the live solver against those tables: root-mean-square error 0.0042 / 0.0043 (for $u$ / $v$ profiles) at $\mathrm{Re}=1000$, against a pass threshold of 0.02.

▶ run the cavity

One more omission is mathematical rather than physical. Whether smooth solutions of the 3-D Navier–Stokes equations always exist for all time is an open problem — one of the Clay Millennium Prize questions. In 2-D, existence and uniqueness are settled theorems, so the equations behind this lab are on rigorous footing; the solver’s errors are honest discretization, not mathematical quicksand.

Appendix A. Why the projection works — and why it is the smallest fix

Section §6 asserted two properties of the projection step $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}^* - \nabla\varphi$: that it lands exactly on the constraint, and that it disturbs the provisional field as little as possible. Both deserve proof. Throughout, fields live on the lab’s rectangular domain and satisfy the solver’s boundary conditions: either the normal velocity component is fixed on the boundary (walls, inflow) or the domain is periodic; $\varphi$ correspondingly gets a zero normal derivative (“Neumann”) or periodic condition, matching the discrete solver’s treatment.

Theorem A.1 (projection). Let $\mathbf{u}^*$ be any smooth velocity field and let $\varphi$ solve the Poisson equation $\nabla^2\varphi = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}^*$ with the boundary conditions above. Set $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}^* - \nabla\varphi$. Then: (i) $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} = 0$; (ii) among all divergence-free fields $\mathbf{z}$ with the same boundary flux, $\mathbf{u}$ is the one closest to $\mathbf{u}^*$ in the mean-square sense $\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 = \int |\mathbf{w}|^2\,dA$: $$\boxed{\;\|\mathbf{u}^* - \mathbf{u}\| \;\le\; \|\mathbf{u}^* - \mathbf{z}\| \quad\text{for every admissible divergence-free } \mathbf{z}.\;}\tag{A.1}$$
(i) is a computation with Definition 2.7: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}^* - \nabla\cdot(\nabla\varphi) = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}^* - \nabla^2\varphi = 0$ by the choice of $\varphi$. (ii) The key fact is that a gradient is orthogonal to every admissible divergence-free field, in the integral sense: for any $\mathbf{w}$ with $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{w}=0$ and zero normal component on the boundary (the difference of two admissible fields has zero flux), $$\int \nabla\varphi\cdot\mathbf{w}\;dA = \int \big[\nabla\cdot(\varphi\,\mathbf{w}) - \varphi\,(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{w})\big]\,dA = \oint \varphi\,(\mathbf{w}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}})\,d\ell - 0 = 0,$$ using the product rule for divergence, then the divergence theorem (the integral of $\nabla\cdot(\varphi\mathbf{w})$ over the domain equals the flux of $\varphi\mathbf{w}$ through the boundary — Proposition 2.4 summed over all boxes, interior faces cancelling in pairs), and finally $\mathbf{w}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}=0$ on the boundary (or cancellation across periodic edges). Now take any admissible $\mathbf{z}$ and expand, writing $\mathbf{u}^*-\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{u}^*-\mathbf{u}) + (\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{z}) = \nabla\varphi + (\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{z})$: $$\|\mathbf{u}^*-\mathbf{z}\|^2 = \|\nabla\varphi\|^2 + \|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{z}\|^2 + 2\!\int\!\nabla\varphi\cdot(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{z})\,dA = \|\mathbf{u}^*-\mathbf{u}\|^2 + \|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{z}\|^2,$$ because $\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{z}$ is divergence-free with zero boundary flux, killing the cross term. The right-hand side is smallest — equal to $\|\mathbf{u}^*-\mathbf{u}\|^2$ — exactly when $\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{u}$, which is (A.1). ∎

This is the Pythagorean theorem in a space whose “vectors” are whole velocity fields: the projection drops a perpendicular from $\mathbf{u}^*$ onto the constraint set. It also explains Fig. 10’s visual: the arrows barely change because the correction $\nabla\varphi$ is the minimum the constraint allows.
